# Backgrounder document
In Nova Scotia, where rental vacancy rates and housing demand continue to threaten market stability, housing affordability remains a crucial concern. The province’s purpose-built rental vacancy rate increased to roughly 3.1% in the 2025 Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation Rental Market Report, up from around 2.1% in 2024 (Rental Market Report, 2025). However, this rate is still below long-term historical averages, suggesting ongoing supply constraints relative to demand. In 2025, average rents across Nova Scotia also increased, surpassing income growth for many households, especially in lower-cost rental market categories. This indicates continued pressure on affordability for both first-time buyers and renters throughout the province.

The primary policy choice facing provincial and municipal planners in Nova Scotia is whether to continue relying on suburban and greenfield construction to increase housing supply or to prioritize zoning changes that allow for higher-density development within established communities. In addition to making greater use of existing infrastructure—such as roads, water, and sewage systems—higher-density housing options like duplexes and mid-rise residential buildings are often associated with lower municipal service costs per unit. However, density-focused plans may encounter strong public opposition, as well as legal and regulatory barriers that limit adoption and delay implementation (Government of Canada, Statistics Canada, 2026).

On the other hand, suburban greenfield development can deliver new housing more quickly and at a larger scale, particularly in fast-growing regions of the province. However, it often imposes significant long-term infrastructure costs on governments. These expenses—including the construction and maintenance of new transportation networks and public services across dispersed development areas—could strain Nova Scotia’s long-term fiscal sustainability if revenues do not offset infrastructure liabilities. For elected officials and planners across the province, this decision is complex due to the trade-offs between affordability, infrastructure efficiency, environmental sustainability, and community acceptance (Home, n.d.).

This decision affects a wide range of stakeholders. Current homeowners may be concerned about neighbourhood character changes or increased density near their properties, while renters and prospective homeowners across Nova Scotia are directly impacted by housing availability and affordability. Provincial and municipal officials must consider infrastructure costs, regulatory implications, and public interests when developing housing strategies. Developers respond to zoning regulations and land-use policies that shape investment decisions (Home, n.d.). Stakeholders in the transportation and environmental sectors also have a vested interest, as low-density expansion may increase car dependency, whereas more compact growth patterns tend to support public transit use and reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

The Province of Nova Scotia has begun developing broader housing strategies to address persistent affordability challenges, including participation in the federal Housing Accelerator Fund. These efforts aim to align municipal planning reforms with provincial affordability objectives while creating more supportive regulatory conditions for a diverse housing supply (Home, n.d.). Over the next decade, decisions about how and where new housing is built will significantly influence Nova Scotia’s housing affordability, urban development patterns, and financial sustainability. With rents rising and vacancy rates still below historical averages (Rental Market Report, 2025), the long-term implications of these policy choices are critical to equity and economic resilience across the province.
